# CHOG HIDEOUT
## World and Art Bible — Rooftop Mischief

**Version:** 0.1 · 14 October 2026  
**Status:** Proposed game universe and production direction  
**Experience:** A colourful 2D adventure built around short, active raids, expressive movement, and character-owned hideouts.

> A city of beautiful trouble. Someone has decided to put it away.

## 1. What this document establishes

The chosen visual direction is **Rooftop Mischief**: warm terracotta rooftops, expressive purple Chogs, strange homemade inventions, soft lilac distances, and clear, inviting platforming routes. The approved concept image in the conversation is the visual reference. It is an art target, not a screenshot of implemented gameplay.

Chog's official website describes the character as curious and mischievous and refers to the Chog World Order. That is the established starting point. The city, Curios, supporting characters, antagonist, districts, and events below are original proposals for this game. They are not presented as official Chog canon or an endorsed expansion.

**Working product title:** Chog Hideout. It describes the central social feature without locking the wider universe to one activity. The final game and city names can be chosen after the first chapter establishes their personality.

**Design promise:** Players should come for a satisfying escape, remember the Chog who made it, and return to explore a place worth caring about.

## 2. The world in one paragraph

The Burrows is a sprawling neighbourhood of crooked rooftops, pulley bridges, kettle chimneys, and homes built around peculiar objects called Curios. A Curio might brew a cloud, persuade a door to open somewhere unexpected, or keep a room warm with bottled afternoon sunlight. Chogs discover these objects, invent questionable uses for them, and build elaborate hideouts to protect their favourites. But a former city repairer known as the Curator has begun confiscating Curios and connecting whole streets to his Quiet Engine. He promises safety. Behind him, wind chimes stop ringing, windows lose their warmth, and neighbourhoods become perfectly still. A missing possession draws your Chog into a series of break-ins that uncover what his museum is really collecting.

## 3. The emotional experience

The player should feel **capable, curious, mischievous, and increasingly attached**.

Early satisfaction comes from a well-timed jump and a narrow escape. Attachment grows through familiar doors, recurring neighbours, remembered rivalries, and changes in the neighbourhood after successful missions. The wider conflict gives those small actions a reason to matter.

The stakes begin with home. The opening does not ask players to rescue the universe before they have met anyone living in it.

Humour comes from intentions meeting consequences: an overconfident courier caught by their own trap, a security notice that politely refuses to explain anything, a contraption that works beautifully for the wrong purpose. Characters take their problems seriously. The game allows their solutions to be absurd.

### Tone boundaries

- Warm adventure with moments of danger and wonder.
- Cheeky behaviour with visible consequences and opportunities to repair mistakes.
- Brief, characterful dialogue; scenery and action carry most exposition.
- Rivalry that can become friendship.
- Cartoon setbacks and recoveries; no graphic injury.
- No jokes that require crypto knowledge to understand the story.

## 4. Six rules of the universe

### Rule 1 — Curios have specific, limited effects

Every important Curio gets one memorable function and a constraint. A cloud kettle needs pressure and can launch a character only when its valve opens. A lantern may reveal hidden ink but cannot reveal every secret in the world. Established abilities cannot change simply to solve a scene.

### Rule 2 — Chogs make things happen

Characters explore, improvise, compete, and build. The world responds to what they do. They are not silent decorations waiting for a quest dispenser to activate them.

### Rule 3 — Mischief can be generous or careless

A prank may create a useful shortcut or damage someone's carefully maintained bridge. The story recognises the difference. Helping neighbours and learning control are part of growing into a skilled raider.

### Rule 4 — Stillness is imposed, not a punishment for being quiet

Quiet characters and peaceful places belong in the world. The Curator's threat is removing people's freedom to use, change, and enjoy their possessions. The game does not equate introversion, tidiness, or caution with villainy.

### Rule 5 — Gameplay remains fair

Scenery can surprise; collision rules cannot. A dangerous surface must look dangerous. A safe landing must support the same movement rules as every other safe landing. Decorative objects cannot disguise unavoidable damage.

### Rule 6 — Story raids and friendly challenges have different stakes

Story raids recover confiscated possessions. Community hideout challenges are consensual contests. A successful challenger earns a record or trophy impression; they do not permanently take another player's NFT, possessions, or accumulated progress.

## 5. Curios, Sparks, and trophies

| Element | Meaning in the world | Purpose in play |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Curio** | A peculiar object with a particular effect and a personal history | Mission objective, environmental mechanic, or story milestone |
| **Spark** | A little residue of an active Curio's energy | Optional collectible marking an interesting route or rewarding exploration |
| **Trophy impression** | A harmless copy of a hideout's challenge seal | Proof that a player completed a particular challenge |
| **Quiet seal** | The Curator's numbered confiscation mark | Signals his involvement and distinguishes his spaces visually |

Sparks are proposed game collectibles. They are not $CHOG or a promised tradeable asset. Their first purpose is making a route satisfying to read and complete.

### First Curio: the Pocket Sun

A palm-sized lantern containing a warm patch of afternoon light. It lights the player's home and makes the windows recognisable from distant rooftops. Its emotional importance comes from familiarity, not a claim that it is the most powerful object in existence.

Its disappearance leaves a small, visible absence: one dark window in a lively street.

### Other Curios for later development

- **Cloud Kettle:** vents a short, predictable updraft when pressure reaches its mark.
- **Borrowed Second:** a clock mechanism that briefly slows a specific moving machine; it cannot stop the entire world.
- **Echo Bell:** repeats the last nearby sound and can distract a particular security device.
- **Wrong-Way Compass:** points toward the last place its owner deliberately avoided; useful for a character-driven side story.

Only the Cloud Kettle is a candidate for the opening slice. The others are world-development material, not a first-build feature list.

## 6. The people we remember

The game treats participating Chog NFTs as individual playable characters. Supporting cast designs and names below are provisional and should not be attributed to existing holders or official team members.

| Character | Want and contradiction | Role in the opening | Visual and performance notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Your Chog** | Wants their possession back; discovers a talent for getting into trouble and out of it | Playable lead whose identity comes from the selected character | Preserve the actual selected Chog's recognisable face and traits; expressive eyes, hands, posture, and motion |
| **Pip, the tinkerer** | Wants every invention to work; keeps discovering a better use after it fails | Provides the first practical clue and introduces the dash through action | Oversized work gloves, a small brass tool belt, bright attentive poses; talks quickly but gives short instructions |
| **Moss, the route keeper** | Wants people to get home safely; secretly enjoys a brilliantly executed shortcut | Establishes neighbourhood stakes and the difference between brave and careless | Patched ochre coat, broad grounded silhouette, economical movement; dry humour |
| **Rook, the courier** | Wants to be seen as the best; cannot bring himself to abandon a struggling rival | Demonstrates the friendly challenge tradition and later becomes a recurring rival | Asymmetric satchel, distinctive pink accent, forward-leaning stance; showy arrivals, awkward sincerity |
| **The Curator** | Wants to prevent another neighbourhood accident; believes control is the only reliable protection | Antagonist whose confiscations connect the missions | Long cream coat, dark gloves, numbered brass tags; precise movements and restrained geometric shapes |
| **Latch, an inspector** | Wants every delivery to match its paperwork; struggles with ordinary improvisation | Small mechanical obstacle and source of environmental comedy | Round brass housing, visible hinge, one inspection lens; clear wind-up before action |

Pip, Moss, and Rook should have their own needs and relationships. They cannot exist only to praise or instruct the player.

### Voice examples

**Pip:** “It was meant to boil water. The roof came off. We kept the useful part.”

**Moss:** “A shortcut gets you there sooner. That was a hole.”

**Rook:** “You can follow me. Keeping up is a separate arrangement.”

**The Curator:** “Your possession is safe. Your access to it was the unsafe part.”

These are tone samples, not final recorded dialogue.

## 7. The Curator's argument

Before becoming the Curator, he repaired structures damaged by unstable inventions. One serious accident convinced him that goodwill and improvisation were inadequate safeguards.

His museum began as temporary storage for genuinely dangerous objects. The temporary labels never came off. The Quiet Engine then gave him a way to suppress irregular motion around those objects and, eventually, across occupied streets.

His concern has a basis. His solution removes choice, community, and ownership from everyone else.

The player's answer is demonstrated through careful action: learn how an object works, make a safe route, repair what was damaged, and return something to the person who values it. The eventual resolution should involve responsibility as well as escape.

Avoid an early speech explaining this backstory. Introduce it through repair marks beneath museum labels, old photographs, and what Moss remembers. The full explanation belongs later.

## 8. A world with distinct districts

The city is navigated through a compact illustrated map. A broad universe can be suggested through distant routes before every district becomes playable.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

All districts use the same character proportions, surface conventions, interface language, and underlying illustration style. Lighting and materials vary without making them look like unrelated games.

## 9. Hideout culture

A Chog's hideout is part home, part workshop, and part expression of personality. Neighbours have a tradition: leave a challenge seal by the entrance, name the rules, and invite others to reach it.

The invitation matters. It distinguishes a friendly raid from trespass and makes competitive play consistent with the warmer tone.

A memorable hideout communicates its owner's personality through layout and a few deliberate details. A show-off might put the trophy in full view with a difficult route. A patient builder might design a sequence that rewards observation. A generous rival might leave a difficult optional route beside an accessible main exit.

### Community challenge rules

1. The owner must complete the exact published layout under its declared movement rules.
2. The room advertises required abilities before the attempt.
3. Safe spawn and exit zones cannot be trapped.
4. Trophy records refer to a particular layout version; editing a room does not rewrite old achievements.
5. A visitor's failure does not destroy their belongings.
6. Repeated attempts remain quick and understandable.

An owner clear proves possibility, not fairness. Rooms still need limits on hazard density, landing space, and misleading geometry.

## 10. What the NFT contributes

**The NFT represents a particular Chog in this game.** It is not proposed as a second invented population of disposable characters. The relationship between a collection item and the game's character must be explicit.

| Layer | Proposed relationship to the NFT | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Playable identity | Actual character art and verified traits inform its game appearance | Do not silently substitute a generic mascot for every NFT |
| Hideout control | The current verified owner can edit that character's active hideout | An old owner loses future editing authority after ownership changes |
| Public history | Versioned raids, defences, and earned distinctions are associated with collection contract plus token ID | Historical records distinguish the character from the controller at the time |
| Guest participation | An owner can authorise use of the character in a limited game context | Guest permission does not transfer or approve spending the NFT |
| Ownership changes | A new owner can claim current in-game control and retain the visible character history | Private messages, personal account information, and other users' data do not transfer |

### Fairness principle

Movement fundamentals and the core story should not be stronger simply because a character is expensive or rare. Appearance and balanced optional styles can create identity without selling a better jump.

### Visitor access

Allow a short introductory raid without wallet friction. Sustained character-owned play can use verified ownership or an owner-authorised guest roster. Public guest availability needs a defined policy; it must not assume permission from every holder.

### What we can honestly claim

The current prototype has no NFT integration. A later build can claim verified ownership, owner-controlled hideouts, or guest delegation only after those features exist and are tested. A database indexed by token ID remains dependent on the game's service; it is not automatically immutable, decentralised, or interoperable.

The proposed ownership loop needs demonstration in the submission: two characters with distinct owners and rooms, a guest permission boundary, and a verified change of control. Lore alone does not satisfy NFT essentiality.

## 11. First story arc — The Missing Light

The opening arc stays in and around the Burrows. Each mission changes something the player has already seen.

| Beat | What the player does | What they learn | Visible consequence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. One Dark Window** | Return along a familiar roof route and investigate home | The Pocket Sun is missing; someone left an official-looking receipt | Their home window stays dark |
| **2. Follow the Brass Tags** | Follow a marked delivery route; learn the dash from a safe crossing | Pip recognises the confiscation tags and points toward a courier store | A previously closed workshop becomes a friendly stop |
| **3. Rook's Shortcut** | Complete a short consensual hideout challenge | Rivalry is part of neighbourhood life; Rook has seen the collectors | Rook marks an optional route on the map |
| **4. The Quiet Street** | Cross a street affected by the Quiet Engine and bypass an inspector | More than possessions are being taken; the neighbourhood is changing | One small street mechanism restarts after intervention |
| **5. The First Vault** | Recover the Pocket Sun from a local collection depot and escape | The confiscations lead to a larger museum network | The home window lights again; the next district is revealed |

This is the chapter outline. The first polished build targets **one coherent opening raid**, compressing the necessary introduction and first recovery into a short complete experience. It does not require producing five separate finished levels before the game can be shown.

### Opening scene

The rooftops are busy with ordinary life: a pulley basket rises, a kettle chimney puffs, and a neighbour's washing flutters. The player's home is the one window without a warm glow.

A brass receipt hangs from its door:

> ITEM REMOVED FOR SAFEKEEPING.  
> RETURN PERIOD: INDEFINITE.

The Chog looks from the receipt to a departing collector's cart. A faint warm light leaks from one parcel. The cart disappears beyond the next roof.

Control begins immediately. The first platform is safe, and the first jump teaches movement without a fatal gap.

The introduction should communicate loss and direction in approximately ten seconds, with a skip option. The player should not have to read a lore screen before moving.

### Ending the opening raid

The recovered Pocket Sun returns warmth to the home window. The camera briefly reveals other dark windows farther across the city. Rook appears on a nearby roof, holding another brass receipt.

The local problem is resolved. The larger problem becomes personal and visible.

## 12. The polished opening slice

**Playable objective:** Recover the Pocket Sun and reach the escape door.

**Primary verbs:** Move, jump, dash, collect, observe.

**Environment interactions:** Automatic proximity interactions for the first slice; do not add a separate interact button unless a meaningful choice requires it.

### Route structure

1. Safe entry with room to discover movement.
2. One readable jump with a forgiving landing.
3. A dash demonstration with a generous recovery area.
4. A choice between a direct route and an optional collectible route.
5. A short combination of already taught actions.
6. Clear recovery of the objective, escape, and brief home payoff.

**Pacing target:** A clean learned run may be considerably shorter than a first attempt. Design for a satisfying short session; do not stretch a level merely to advertise a 60-second runtime.

### Explicit level-design corrections

The prototype's wide gap followed immediately by spikes is a known fairness defect. Shorten or retune the crossing and provide a visibly safe landing before asking for another action. Validate a range of jump and dash timings, not just one scripted successful path.

Do not require near-frame-perfect input in the opening. Later optional challenges may demand precision after players have learned the controls.

### Failure and retry

Failure clearly identifies its cause. The character has a quick expressive recovery animation rather than a violent death. Retry returns control promptly, without wallet prompts, advertisements, or an unrelated results screen.

If a scene displays hearts, define an actual health system. The generated concepts show illustrative hearts; the prototype currently uses immediate failure. Production UI must reflect whichever rule is deliberately chosen.

## 13. Art bible — Rooftop Mischief

### Visual sentence

**An exuberant illustrated rooftop city, built from warm handmade materials, where purple characters cause small spectacular problems.**

The environment should feel inhabited before a mission starts. One identifiable home detail is worth more than a wall covered in interchangeable decoration.

### Colour roles

| Role | Direction | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Character emphasis | Saturated Chog purple and pink, with the selected NFT's verified variations | Maintain a clean silhouette and recognisable identity |
| Playable surfaces | Warm terracotta, ochre, cream edge highlights | Identify supported landing planes |
| Depth | Muted lilac, pale blue, restrained mint | Separate background from action |
| Rewards and objectives | Selective amber or warm gold | Guide attention without filling the screen with glow |
| Curator influence | Ivory, desaturated brass, cool grey, numbered geometric marks | Establish an orderly visual intrusion |
| Danger | Sharp silhouettes, mechanical movement, strong local contrast | Never rely on red alone |

These are colour roles, not a demand that every scene use equal quantities of every colour. Actual NFT art can require local background adjustments for contrast.

### Five environment layers

1. **Sky and distant atmosphere:** low detail and slow parallax.
2. **Distant city:** broad roof and tower shapes; suggest routes beyond the current level.
3. **Near neighbourhood:** selected windows, bridges, and readable life details.
4. **Gameplay plane:** crisp top edges, hazards, doors, characters, and interactive props.
5. **Foreground framing:** sparse silhouettes at the edges; never hide a jump, landing, enemy, or collectible.

Platforming uses a consistent side-on collision plane. Painted perspective can create depth, but cannot imply a landing surface the player cannot actually stand on.

### Shape language

- Chog spaces: rounded doors, asymmetry, repaired timber, curved pipes, friendly chunky forms.
- The Curator: repeating modules, straight rails, aligned tags, measured spacing, restrained ornament.
- Hazards: deliberate hard points, clear motion cues, consistent contact surfaces.
- Safe edges: highlighted horizontal surfaces with visible support beneath them.

### Environmental storytelling

A child's height marks beside a door; a repaired bridge bearing Moss's patch; Rook's delivery badge attached backwards; matching confiscation tags on very different homes. Such details make recurring places memorable without adding explanatory paragraphs.

Avoid scattering arbitrary gears, barrels, foliage, and signs simply to increase apparent detail.

## 14. Character production and movement

The official Chog reference used for concept exploration has a purple spiky silhouette, a pale expressive face, and pink accents. That reference does not define the appearance of every individual NFT.

Before animation, prepare front, side, and three-quarter model views for the chosen playable character. Lock proportions, outline weight, facial features, lighting direction, and trait attachments. Avoid changing those features between generated frames.

### Minimum animation set for the opening

| State | What must read |
| --- | --- |
| Idle | Alert curiosity through breathing, eyes, and a small posture shift |
| Run | Weight, foot contact, direction, and a strong silhouette |
| Jump start and rise | Clear lift without delaying the player's input |
| Apex and fall | A readable transition that helps players judge distance |
| Land | A brief compression and recovery; no forced pause in movement |
| Dash | A distinct forward pose, restrained trail, and visible cooldown feedback |
| Hit or fall recovery | Cause of failure and character personality |
| Success | A short, characterful reaction that does not block replay |

Animation follows the simulation. Squash, stretch, clothing, and trails must not unpredictably move collision boundaries. A visual anticipation pose cannot introduce unresponsive input delay.

### Camera rules

Use modest forward look-ahead and a stable vertical frame. Show a gap and its landing before the player commits. Keep camera motion independent from visual effects. Screen shake, flashes, and motion effects should be restrained and reducible.

## 15. Interface and mobile play

The game should occupy the main viewing area. Menus and surrounding pages support entering, continuing, and understanding play.

### In-play interface

- Small location label when entering a district; allow it to recede afterward.
- A concise objective when needed.
- Collectible count if collectibles have a defined role in the slice.
- Dash availability near the ability button.
- Pause and restart access.
- Touch controls that stay outside the central route and do not conceal landings.

Landscape is the preferred play composition. Portrait remains usable or offers a clear landscape suggestion; do not depend on forced orientation support. Touch targets should be comfortably sized, with simultaneous movement and jump/dash input supported.

Pause when focus is lost. Never continue a dangerous run behind a menu or hidden tab.

Story scenes should be short and skippable. Essential information also appears in readable text; sound cannot be its only carrier.

## 16. Sound and feedback

### The Burrows sound palette

Light percussion, plucked wood or strings, small metallic accents, distant neighbourhood activity, cloth, and occasional kettle puffs. Keep room for useful action sounds rather than filling every moment with music.

### Action feedback

- Jump: short, soft departure sound and a small dust cue.
- Dash: quick air snap with a distinct ready-again cue.
- Landing: material-specific contact; gentle visual compression.
- Spark: a clear bright tone with small variation; collecting several feels musical.
- Danger: an identifiable mechanical cue before an active hazard triggers.
- Recovery: short and light; no loud punishment sting on every attempt.
- Curio return: a recognisable warm musical phrase that can recur at home.

Sound should make successful timing satisfying. It must not disguise latency or interrupt immediate retry. Provide separate music and effects controls and a persistent mute option.

## 17. Asset and technical direction

**Recommended production direction:** Phaser with TypeScript for a browser-first 2D game. The current experiment uses custom JavaScript and Canvas; the production migration has not been completed.

Keep the game simulation, character rendering, level data, interface, and ownership checks separable. Wallet work must not run inside the per-frame movement loop. Shared ownership and room permissions require a server-side authority; browser-local settings are insufficient for that claim.

### Asset deliverables for one slice

| Asset group | Minimum usable output |
| --- | --- |
| Player | Consistent model reference, sprite atlas, animation timings, origin points, collision reference |
| Background | Separately exportable sky, far-city, near-city, and sparse foreground layers |
| Environment | Repeatable platform pieces, corner treatments, walls, one door, a small set of meaningful props |
| Interaction | Spark, Pocket Sun, spike trap, and any chosen dash teaching prop |
| Effects | Dash trail, landing dust, collection cue, restrained door/Curio effects |
| UI | A small consistent icon set with normal, pressed, unavailable, and focus states |
| Audio | Short licensed or original effects, ambience loop, music loop, and return motif |

A generated gameplay concept is a reference for these separate assets. It must not become a flat background with unrelated boxes placed over its painted platforms. Collision geometry and visible surfaces are authored together.

### Performance targets to verify

Aim for responsive 60 fps on representative devices; measure rather than promise it. Use fixed-step or otherwise frame-independent simulation, compressed assets, atlases, and a deliberately small initial download. Test a midrange Android phone as well as desktop. Effects and background motion should scale down before input responsiveness does.

## 18. Production order

### Gate A — Lock the opening story and visual rules

Agree the Curio, first route, supporting character roles, and Rooftop Mischief model sheets. Resolve how the selected NFT's actual appearance becomes an animated character.

### Gate B — Fix and validate movement

Correct the unfair landing in the current prototype. Test multiple plausible jump and dash timings, touch combinations, focus loss, retry, and camera visibility. This remains a playable control test while art is developed.

### Gate C — Finish one art-complete raid

Implement one environment, one consistent animated character, a short opening and payoff, sound, and clear results. Test that the playable scene retains the readability of the approved concept.

### Gate D — Demonstrate ownership with a real interaction

Verify the collection and token identity, enforce current-owner editing, create a character's hideout, and let a permitted visitor complete its challenge. Test permission expiry or ownership change. Add only the ownership features that can be demonstrated reliably.

### Gate E — Add a reason to return

Improve optional routes, personal bests, and a small set of genuinely good challenges. Observe voluntary retries before adding currencies, elaborate progression, or more districts.

The wider campaign remains an outline until the opening slice and ownership loop work.

## 19. Acceptance questions

### Story

- Can a new player explain what was taken, who took it, and why they are following?
- Does at least one recurring character have a memorable want beyond helping the player?
- Does completing the raid visibly improve a place the player recognises?

### Gameplay

- Can a player understand their failure without an explanation from the developer?
- Can they land safely after the required dash using a reasonable range of timings?
- Can they retry and regain control promptly on touch and keyboard?
- Is the optional route a real choice rather than the only way to finish?

### Visuals

- Are character, hazards, and supported surfaces readable on a phone?
- Does moving gameplay preserve the approved concept's identity?
- Do animation frames maintain the same face, proportions, and traits?
- Are important surfaces unobscured by controls, foreground art, and effects?

### Ownership

- Is the chosen Chog an actual verified collection item?
- Is there a meaningful action only its current owner can authorise?
- Can visitors participate within an explicit permission boundary?
- Are public character history and private player data kept distinct?

### Evidence

Use actual play observations, not invented retention percentages. Record what testers tried, where they stopped, and whether they voluntarily replayed. Scripted completion demonstrates possibility; it does not establish enjoyable difficulty.

## 20. Decisions still open

1. Final game and city names; Chog Hideout and the district names are working labels.
2. Which collection and character can be used in the first integrated demonstration, and what art assets are available.
3. Whether the first production slice uses single-hit recovery or a clearly implemented heart system.
4. Exact guest-access policy and how control follows a verified ownership change.
5. Which story elements align with any additional official Chog lore supplied by the team.

These decisions do not require redesigning the chosen visual direction. They should be resolved before dependent implementation.
